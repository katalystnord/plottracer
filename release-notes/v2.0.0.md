PlotTracer 2.0.0 — bar charts become a first-class record

Every digitizer treats a bar chart as a scatter plot you happen to click on the
top of each bar. That is not what a bar is. A bar is an **interval** — it runs
from somewhere to somewhere, and both ends carry meaning. Read as a single
point, a floating bar loses its lower end, a stacked segment reports the total
instead of its own contribution, and a bar below the baseline comes back
positive.

PlotTracer 2.0 records the interval. That is a change to the shape of the
record itself, which is what earns the major version.

**A bar is captured by dragging corner to corner.** Click one corner, a live
rectangle follows the cursor, a second click fixes the opposite corner. The
same gesture captures a plain bar, a floating one, a grouped sub-bar and a
stacked segment — there is no mode to find and nothing to switch.

- **Bars that share a baseline.** Tick *Bars share a baseline* and type its
  value. Each bar is then measured from that baseline to its far end, in value
  space rather than pixel space — which is what makes a bar **below** the
  baseline read negative rather than being flipped by hand.
- **Floating bars.** Untick the baseline and there is nothing to sign against,
  so the drag direction carries the meaning: corner-to-corner up is positive,
  down is negative. A tornado chart or a monthly temperature range works
  directly.
- **Stacked bars.** Each segment is its own drag-box, and naming a *Stack
  group* on the series tells the app they belong together. A stacked segment
  reads as its own span, never as a running total — the one number a
  point-based reading cannot give you.
- **Horizontal bars.** One checkbox. The category axis follows onto Y and
  everything else is unchanged.
- **Log value axes**, and a value axis that does not start at zero.

The names live in one canonical list that every series reads from, so renaming
a category in one place renames it everywhere — and a new series' bars are
prefilled from whichever named bar they actually sit under along the category
axis, not from the order you happened to click. Where the pairing is ambiguous
the cell is left **blank**: a name you fill in yourself is honest, a wrong name
that looks transcribed is not. An unnamed bar shows a dash and is never given
an invented "Bar 1".

The data panel shows one row per category and one column per series, so a
grouped chart reads the way it looks on the page. The old panel showed one
series at a time and hid the rest.

A histogram bin is a drag-box whose two corners are the bin's top edge, so its
height is one measurement rather than an extent. A box plot's five slots stayed
as they were, but the shared tuple language, the shared table and the shared
category list now cover all three types.

**Auto-extract by colour works on bars and histograms.** Sample a bar's colour
and the detector proposes bounding boxes for every bar that matches; the
proposal is yours to accept, correct or discard. It is an assist, not an
oracle, and the measured numbers below say where it stops. It stays declined on
Box Plot, Line and Pie — a box's five letter-values, a categorical point and a
sector are not bounding boxes, and returning a midpoint instead would be a
plausible wrong number rather than an honest refusal.

**Pie charts** gained the same category treatment as bars — a slice's name is
click-to-edit, shows a dash at rest, and is never invented.

**Choosing a graph type is now a card grid** with an icon and a description for
each, rather than a dropdown whose entries you had to already know.

The workflow, the ten tools *with the icons the rail actually draws*, and the
keys worth knowing. It is deliberately small and fits on one screen: it is for
the moment you are three clicks into a calibration and cannot remember which
key nudges, not a tour. **F1** opens it, and so does a button at the top of the
**?** menu, which carries the `F1` hint so the key gets learned rather than
hidden — a shortcut nobody has been told about is a feature that does not exist
for a first-time user. The **?** menu also gained a **Manual** button that opens
the full manual in your browser.

The website now carries recall figures for automatic extraction against two
public academic benchmarks — 1,208 charts across real open-access paper figures
and synthetic ones — together with the misses and what causes them. Hatched
fills and greyscale are named with numbers rather than described as edge cases.
The harness is public.

One correction worth stating plainly: greyscale was previously described as
close to a scope boundary, on the assumption that colour-keyed extraction has
nothing to key on. Measurement showed that is false — 81% of greyscale figures
carry as many distinct shades as they have series. The signal is there; our
ruler was too coarse for it. The claim has been corrected everywhere it
appeared.

This release closes 102 commits' worth of work, and the fixes worth naming are
all one family: **a calibration that reported success on input that made every
reading wrong.**

- A **map scale bar of zero** calibrated cleanly and then measured every
  distance and area as exactly 0.
- **XY and Polar accepted a log scale that cannot be logged**, and Polar and
  CCR reported success on a calibration with no radial range at all.
- A **pie project could reopen with every value NaN** if its stored total was
  anything other than a plain number — the figure and its points looked
  entirely normal.
- An **overflowed curve fit** returned coefficients that were not numbers, and
  the project file quietly rewrote them as zeroes — drawing a flat line through
  y = 0 as though it were the answer. A high-degree polynomial over very large
  x values is now refused, naming the degree, the magnitude, and the two things
  you can change.

Also: a deleted bar never gave its category back, leaving undeletable ghost
rows; a multi-series bar chart exported only one series to all nine formats; a
series name could damage the file it was exported into, three different ways; a
WPD folder name crashed Open Project; deskew lost the outer ring of pixels at
180° and 270°; and Shift-clicking a data point replaced the selection instead of
adding to it.

Installers for Linux (.AppImage, .deb), macOS (Apple Silicon .dmg/.zip) and
Windows (.exe) are attached below. The builds are **unsigned**, so macOS and
Windows will warn on first launch; open from the context menu on macOS, or
choose *More info → Run anyway* on Windows.

PlotTracer works fully offline. Your figures never leave your machine.
