Heatmaps - the commonest figure in the literature, and the first coherent
digitizer workflow for one - plus the colour key's own extent on screen, and the
release where CI started running the tests.

## Heatmaps

A heatmap is the most common figure in the scientific literature - **406,986
articles** contain one - and the one no digitizer has handled coherently. There
have been partial attempts, and people do get numbers out of them by hand, through
ImageJ and similar; what has not existed is a calibrated workflow that produces a
record. It is hard for a reason worth stating plainly - in a heatmap the **colour
is the value**, so a wrong cell looks exactly like a right one. There is no curve
out of place to catch your eye.

**A heatmap is a matrix, and it has three axes.** X, Y, and the colour key. Each
one is independently a *category* or a *value* - gene × sample, treatment × time,
field × field - and all four combinations are published, so all four are asked
for on a row of radio buttons you can see rather than infer.

**Three clicks describe the frame.** Not four: the two axes span one rectangle,
so three of its corners carry the whole transform, and the fourth was always the
same click twice. Each axis's second click also says how many columns or rows the
figure has - the one number a person can read straight off a heatmap.

**The colour key is dragged corner to corner**, and it measures its own thickness
from that drag rather than assuming one. Two more clicks on its printed ticks
give it a scale. Those are two separate measurements on purpose: *"the key's ends
are the minimum and maximum"* is a guess, and on a real figure it is wrong by a
measurable amount - the ramp starts where the ink starts, while the printed
numbers sit wherever the figure's tick machinery put them.

**The grid is detected from the figure's own drawn rules**, and where detection
falls short it says so instead of quietly dividing the rest evenly. Every
boundary is draggable, and boundaries can be added and removed. Nothing is ever
drawn as measured that was not measured.

**Every cell carries its own evidence.** Beside the number: the interval its
colour cannot be told apart from, how far that colour sat off the ramp, how much
of the cell was actually that colour, and whether it sits against the key's limit -
because a cell whose data ran past the key is exact, uniform and **clipped**,
which no amount of colour accuracy can recover.

**You are not the only instrument looking at the figure.** A hatched cell, an
asterisk over the fill, a printed label bleeding into the colour - you may see
what the sampler averages away. So type over any cell and your reading is
recorded exactly as ours is, through the same transform, with no flag calling it
second class. What is stored is a **position on the colour key**, not a number,
so recalibrating the key moves your cells along with everyone else's rather than
leaving them behind to disagree.

Category names travel with the values into the export, the file says which
coordinates are *ordinals* so nobody reads band 3 as 3 mm, and the results panel
is tinted with the figure's own colours so a cell in the table and a cell in the
figure are visibly the same cell. A key drawn as a handful of **discrete bands**
is refused by name, rather than averaged into a number the figure never printed.

**Verified, not asserted.** Values read from the bundled figures agree with their
published ground truth to within **0.4%**. The record itself was checked the
harder way: the most awkward figure we have - unequal cells, both axes
continuous - was **regenerated from the record alone**, with a maximum difference
of **0.0** and every recovered edge exact. A record built from cell centres
instead of bounds was wrong by up to 0.375 data units on the same figure, which
is why this one stores bounds.

## The colour key says what it reads at its two ends

Calibrate a key and its calibrated extent now appears at each end of the bar.

It is the one thing about a colour key you cannot see by looking at it. The same
four clicks on a key printed `10¹` and `10²` describe a span of **3 … 589** read
logarithmically and **−38 … 169** read linearly - and on a concentration, one of
those is impossible while the other is right. The two lines are unmistakable side
by side, where thirty cells of plausible numbers are not.

⚑ **The extent is not a warning and nothing is flagged.** A key that crosses zero
is perfectly ordinary - temperature, anomaly, log-fold change - so this reports
what the calibration measured and leaves the reading to the reader. The numbers
were already being written into every export; now they are on screen too, before
the first cell is read, next to the choice that decides them.

## Fixes

- **A mistyped calibration value can be corrected in place**, during the walk,
  without discarding eight clicks and six numbers to change one digit.
- **Detecting a grid and reading its cells are separate undo steps**, so one
  Ctrl+Z stops taking both.
- **A dragged handle stays on the axis it means something on.** Constrained
  handles drifted off-axis on screen while the model stayed perfectly correct -
  the picture lying while the data told the truth, which is the worst pairing.
- **A cell says which cell it is** - in the table, in the file, and in the export,
  which previously wrote a value × value heatmap's bounds with no way to tell
  which cell was which.
- **A reading is bounded by the key's calibrated area, not by its labelled
  ticks** - the ticks are the calibration, and the ramp legitimately runs past
  them.
- **Grid Removal said what it needs.** It required an image and never mentioned
  it.
- Row names were being filed bottom-up.
- The results panel remembers how wide you made it.

## Under the hood

**CI now runs the tests.** Until this release the pipeline ran a typecheck, a
lint and the packager - so a version could be tagged, built and published with
the whole suite unexecuted anywhere but one machine. 3,377 tests now run on every
build in 28 seconds, and the packaging job will not start without them.

A five-pass audit was run against everything this release added, hardest at reuse
rather than at bugs. It found, among others: one colour written seven ways in
three spellings; one band lookup written three times with silently different
behaviour out of range; a cross-cutting invariant file listing eleven graph types
while the app had twelve; and a cell reading that was bounded at two of its three
entrances, so a hand-edited project file could place a cell on a stretch of key
that has no ink.

A graph type now **joins a registry rather than a dropdown**. Every cross-type
rule iterates that registry, so the next chart type is enrolled in all of them on
the day it is added instead of being absent from each by default.
